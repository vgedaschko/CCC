# Big D or little d? Who decides, and how?

*Erin Meyer, The Culture Map, Ch. 5 "Big D or Little d: Who Decides, and How?" (the Deciding scale). A presentation that decides itself. Cross-Cultural Communication, Prof. Sabrina Hegner, Hochschule Bremen (HSB), 06.01.2027.*

This script is the storyboard for the slides and for the speaking texts written later. Its structure and timing come from the "clarity" concept: a linear default path with low-risk branching and a focus on the exam. It also takes ideas from the other two concepts. From "experience": both decisions happen live before any theory, the rules card is secretly Meyer's own protocol, nemawashi is revealed, and the D/d gesture is used. From "story": strict fact checks, DECISION as stone versus decision as pencil, the two rails, countries as bell curves, the censor bar and the Tokyo table.

> **Format update (07.11.2026):** the deck is built in code (HTML/CSS/JS, runs offline in Chrome/Edge) instead of PowerPoint, with a static PDF and a .pptx export generated from it. The PowerPoint build rules in §2.7–2.8 are superseded by the deck engine in `deck/` (see `deck/README.md`); the slide content, timing and interaction design stay as written. Planning default: two presenters.

## 0. At a glance

**The big idea.** The English word "decision" covers two different rulebooks.
- In consensual cultures, a Decision is a **capital-D promise**. It comes late, after everyone has been consulted, and is then carried out quickly with "no more discussion!".
- In top-down cultures, a decision is a **lowercase-d step**. One person, usually the boss, takes it early. It stays open to revision while it is carried out, so implementation can take a long time (Meyer, Figs. 5.1/5.2).

We do not explain this first. The room lives through it:
- **Minute 1:** a "CEO" decides for everyone in about three seconds, then casually revises the decision.
- **Minute 4:** the whole room has to agree before anything happens. Then we pretend to reopen their decision.
- The two clock bars from those moments then Morph into Meyer's two timelines: *"What you just felt has a name."*

Everything after that explains a feeling the audience already had: the US–German merger paradox, the Fig. 5.3 map, Per's inbox, Tokyo's ringi, and Meyer's toolkit.

Why it should stick:
- It is physical: people stood up, were overruled, and were asked to give up their decision.
- It fits into one letter (D or d).
- It comes with an exam mnemonic **(ours)**: consensual = *slow in, fast out*; top-down = *fast in, slow out (flexible)*.
- It ends on a question classmates can reuse in any team: *"So — was that a big D or a little d?"*

## 1. Team & roles

### 1.1 Principles

**Roles and people**
- **Roles A, B and C cover Part I and the shared Part II slides** (S15, S18, S19, S20).
  - Assign roles once you know who is presenting.
  - Rotate them in the drills (§1.5) so anyone can take any block.
- **The own-culture slides belong to people, not roles.**
  - P1, P2 and P3 belong to Member 1, 2 and 3, in a fixed order.
  - Each member presents their own slide, whatever role they hold.

**Slides are the cue cards (no notes are allowed)**
- **Rules for every slide:**
  - a headline of at most 9 words
  - one visual
  - at most **4 quote fragments of at most 12 words each** (emails and drafts shown as props in S04 and S09 do not count)
  - a breadcrumb that names the rubric item
  - at most 6 clicks
- **Quote rule:** speak verbatim only what is on screen ([SCREEN] in §3). Everything else is a paraphrase cue, spoken as "Meyer reports that…". This way nobody memorises quotes, and nothing is misquoted in front of the professor.
- **Click counts:** every slide in §3 lists its clicks. Anyone covering a block learns them in the drills.

**Live roles**
- **A** sits at the laptop during both decisions. A uses the clicker for builds and the mouse only for small labelled hotspots, and runs the Presenter View timer (§2.9).
- **B** plays the "CEO" in Decision #1 (a few lines) and facilitates Decision #2 (the hardest live moment).

**Complexity is balanced, not just minutes**
- A carries the conceptual core: the definition and timelines, the Leading/Deciding paradox, the mixed-team protocol, and (with two presenters) Tokyo, ringi and the exam card.
- B carries the live facilitation, the merger quotes, the map and the whole toolkit.
- With three presenters, C carries the case stories (collision and roots, Per, Tokyo, ringi) and the apply/exam block.

### 2.2 Decision #1: top-down (S02)

**Script, step by step**
1. S02 Morphs in from the title, showing two tiles, **NEW YORK | MUNICH**, and the headline *Whose story first?* A small chip "MUNICH → 3" sits at the bottom edge.
2. **A:** "Before we explain anything, this presentation has to decide where it starts: New York or Munich. Let's hear what you—"
3. **B** steps forward and A clicks (clicker): the CEO badge pops up and an amber sliver "#1 ≈ 3 s" wipes in. **B:** "No time. I'm the CEO today. New York." The decision takes about 3 seconds, and nobody is asked.
4. **A:** "Hands up if you would have picked Munich." (2 s) **B:** "Noted. Your input was not solicited. Welcome to top-down." This plants Meyer's top-down recommendation to follow a decision even if your input was not solicited.
5. A clicks: Larry Nicoli's card fades up over the NEW YORK tile. A tells his story (≈ 25 s).
6. **B** interrupts: "Change of plan — new data from Munich. We're switching." **A** sighs: "We'd only just started…"
7. A clicks: the amber **REVISED** banner wipes in, a ↺ appears on the clock sliver, and Matthias Wulf's card fades up over the MUNICH tile. The room watches a little-d revision happen.
8. A clicks: a row of small arrows, already pointing left, wipes in from the right ("March left!"), and a big "?" fades in between the cards.
9. Micro-poll: *"Team Larry or Team Matthias — who is more hierarchical?"* Then: "Hold that vote. In about ten minutes you'll see both are right."

**Branch.** If the CEO says "Munich", A clicks the small chip **"MUNICH → 3"** with the mouse. The tile itself carries no link. This jumps to hidden **slide 3 (S02M)**:
- S02M uses a Fade 0.3 s transition. It starts with the CEO badge and the clock sliver already placed, so it matches what is on screen.
- It plays Munich first, then "REVISED · new data from New York", then Nicoli.
- Fallback: type `3` + Enter.
- From S02 or S02M, the next click lands on S03 (slide 4). From S02, the hidden slide is skipped automatically.

**Fallbacks**
- *The room shouts preferences:* "Thanks for your input" — and decide anyway. This is Meyer's top-down advice: listen, then decide quickly.
- *Nobody raises a hand for Munich:* "Nobody? Then top-down worked perfectly — or nobody dared."
- *The chip misfires:* type `3` + Enter, or simply say "New York" and continue on the default path.

**Involving the professor respectfully (optional).** Do this only if she agreed in the mid-December email (§2.10). That email is itself a little nemawashi.
- A says: "Prof. Hegner, you're the boss today — New York or Munich?"
- If she hesitates for more than 5 seconds, B says: "Then — top-down — I decide."
- The default is always a presenter as CEO. She is the decision-maker, never the joke, and she is **never** asked to vote, stamp or approve anything in Decision #2.

**Fallbacks**
- *Instant unanimity (under 20 s):* "Fast for a consensus. Real agreement, social pressure — or root-binding? Hold that thought." This pays off in S11. Bank the saved time.
- *Shy room:* do a simultaneous count, "3-2-1 — stand if you agree", then "Silence isn't consensus. Hands, please." If people stay seated and say nothing, use the "Round 2 — now" rule above.
  - B may address a nemawashi ally by name **only** if that person agreed to it.
  - A may play devil's advocate for the minority option.
  - Never single out a random quiet student.
- *Chaotic room:* the facilitator repeats concerns instead of letting people argue across the room. The clock decides with no extension, because the deadline was agreed.
- *Tie at the deadline:* "Tie: our rules can't break it, so the facilitator decides — one person, top-down." A clicks no stamp; B states the result.
- *Someone can't or won't stand:* "both hands up" counts the same. Say this in round 1.
- *A stamp trigger fails, or a stray click fires the flip arrow early:* say the result out loud. For an early arrow, A presses ← once to undo the last build. The outcome lives in the room, and S14 is named aloud.
- *The professor:* she is not asked to take part. If she joins spontaneously, she counts as one voice.

### 2.4 Change-of-mind test (end of S04, ≤ 20 s; the same on both paths)
1. **B:** "Great — decision made!" (beat) "Actually… new data just came in. Let's do the other scenario." A clicks, and an amber arrow labelled *new data?* fades in between the cards.
2. Pause for 2 seconds. **B:** "How does that feel?" Take **one** reaction. If nobody reacts within 2 seconds, **B:** "Nobody minds? Martina Müller would have."
3. A clicks, and Müller's line fades in. **A:** "That's Martina Müller, a German director in the merger: [SCREEN] *'They casually change the decision every week…'* Relax — your decision stands. Rule ⑤: no reopening."
4. The payoff comes in S05: "Which flip felt worse — the CEO's or ours? Same move, different rules." This works with or without protests.

### 2.5 D/d gesture and micro-polls
- **The gesture:** Big D = both arms up, wide. Little d = one index finger.
- It is introduced at the end of S05 and used in the S12 quick-fire, the S15 room mapping and the S20 callback.
- The true/false items in S14c use thumbs, so the two signals never get mixed up.
- Micro-polls are listed in §2.1. Each one is a show of hands lasting at most 10 seconds.

### 2.6 How the professor is involved
- *Optional:* she can be "the boss" in Decision #1, only if she agreed beforehand. A presenter takes over after 5 seconds.
- *Never:* she is not part of the consensual vote, the stamp, or the final stand-up.
- In Q&A she is the expert in the room. The backup hub answers questions on course theory.

## 5. Q&A prep

1. **"So is consensus better — or top-down?"**
   - Neither. Meyer says either system can work as long as everyone understands and follows the rules of the game (shown on H2).
   - Consensus buys commitment and fast implementation at the cost of time. Ringi critics add shirked accountability and a race that may already be lost.
   - Top-down buys speed and flexibility at the cost of buy-in and constant revision, so implementation can drag.
   - Meyer herself would have traded group agreement for initial speed. Per saw it the other way. → H2.

2. **"How can the US be egalitarian and top-down at the same time?"**
   - Leading and Deciding measure different things: structure (titles, distance) versus who decides.
   - Meyer: the US combines an egalitarian ethos with a more top-down approach. One person, generally the person in charge, decides quickly for the group, so the US is more top-down than hierarchical.
   - The roots are the pioneers: speed, individualism, and decisions by the leader or by vote.

3. **"And Germany, hierarchical but consensual?"**
   - In many German companies, power is generally vested in a small group of senior managers who manage through group agreement.
   - In larger companies, the Aufsichtsrat appoints the Vorstand, which holds final decision-making responsibility. The chairman therefore has considerably less individual power than in many other countries.
   - Even meeting agendas went round for consensus (Müller).

4. **"Why are Japan and Korea at opposite ends, both in Asia?"**
   - The Country Mapping shows Japan at the extreme consensual end, and Korea and China strongly top-down (S08b). Asia is not one block.
   - For Japan, Meyer explains that both hierarchy and consensual decision making are deeply rooted in the culture (ringi, nemawashi).
   - The chapter does not explain Korea's position, so say that honestly and don't speculate. Add the generalization caveat.

5. **"Decision #2 ended in a vote — doesn't that prove consensus doesn't work?"** (if it happened)
   - It proves Fig. 5.1: consensus costs time. Real consensual organisations invest days (the German agenda went round twice before the meeting), and a ringisho climbs level by level through the hierarchy. Meyer says ringi decisions take a long time. That is not two minutes.
   - Our deadline-plus-vote rule *was* Meyer's mixed-team method, and she lists the vote among the **top-down** techniques. That's why the stamp said VOTE, not D.

6. **"Isn't voting democratic, so consensual?"**
   - Not in Meyer's sense. Consensual means unanimous agreement (the Fig. 5.3 definition).
   - A majority vote overrules the minority, who are expected to follow the majority even if they disagree.
   - That is why it sits on her top-down list and in the American pioneers' toolbox (the leader or a vote).

7. **"Can company culture override national culture?"**
   - Partly. Meyer notes that a top-down decision may come before, during or after a meeting, depending on the organisational culture and the individual.
   - At Astellas, ringi runs on software. One of Mori's colleagues (Astellas) added that some Japanese companies have moved away from the system (H2).
   - National positions are central tendencies; organisations and individuals vary (Bennett).

8. **"How does this relate to Hofstede and GLOBE?"** → H4.
   - **No Hofstede dimension sorts Deciding.**
     - The course script lists Sweden as weak in uncertainty avoidance, yet it is ultra-consensual. It lists South Korea as strong in uncertainty avoidance, yet it is strongly top-down.
     - The script's Greece example ties high uncertainty avoidance to consensus, yet Greece sits on the top-down side of the Country Mapping.
     - Its individualist list includes Sweden, the Netherlands and Germany (consensual) and the US and France (top-down). China and Japan, both listed as collectivist, sit at opposite ends.
     - Closest is Consensus Orientation (formerly Femininity, pp. 20–21): every country on its consensus-oriented list that Meyer maps (Sweden, Norway, the Netherlands, Denmark, Finland; §4.3 zones) sits on her consensual side — but Japan heads its "Decisive" list and is ultra-consensual on Meyer's scale.
     - That is why Meyer measures Deciding separately.
   - **GLOBE: the words fit, the clusters split.** Institutional Collectivism contrasts decisions made by group consensus with decisions made by individuals in power (p. 26). But its cluster table (p. 28) rates Confucian Asia high (Japan, but also China and South Korea, which are top-down on Meyer's scale) and Germanic Europe low (Germany and the Netherlands, which are consensual). Nordic Europe high and Latin Europe low do match. Shown on H4.
   - Power Distance is no shortcut either: the script lists Germany and Austria as small power distance (p. 18), while Meyer's Leading scale puts Germany on the hierarchical side.

9. **"Where would you put [country X]?"** → H3.
   - Open the Country Mapping region page, read the dot, and answer relatively: "more consensual than…, more top-down than…".
   - If the country is not mapped, say so.

10. **"Wasn't talking to classmates before class manipulative?"** (if nemawashi was used)
    - We asked for genuine preferences, let them change our proposal, gave no scripts, and revealed it openly.
    - That is exactly nemawashi: informal groundwork that builds commitment. Sheldon's lesson was to do it well before the actual meeting.

## 6. Exam-ready recap: five things to remember

1. **The definitions (verbatim, Fig. 5.3).**
   - Consensual: "Decisions are made in groups through unanimous agreement."
   - Top-down: "Decisions are made by individuals (usually the boss)."
   - The core question: *who decides, and how?*
2. **Big D vs little d (Figs. 5.1/5.2).**
   - Consensual: long discussion → a fixed **Decision** → rapid implementation, "no more discussion!"
   - Top-down: a quick, early **decision** by one person → flexible → "more discussion, possible revisiting and altering of decision" → continual revision, so **implementation can take a long time**.
   - Mnemonic (ours): *slow in, fast out* versus *fast in, slow out (flexible)*.
   - True/false trap: "top-down means fast implementation" is FALSE.
   - When the systems collide: misunderstandings, inefficiency, frustration.
3. **Leading ≠ Deciding (the paradoxes).**
   - Usually egalitarian goes with consensual (Sweden, the Netherlands), and hierarchical goes with top-down ("from Morocco to Korea").
   - Exceptions:
     - The **US** is egalitarian yet top-down.
     - **Germany** is hierarchical yet consensual.
     - **Japan** is hierarchical yet ultra-consensual (ringi + nemawashi).
     - **Vietnam** is similar in the Country Mapping 2022.
   - The merger clash: each side called the other "hierarchical" because each was reading a different scale.
4. **Classification (Fig. 5.3, in clusters from left to right).**
   - Sweden & Japan · Netherlands, Germany · UK · the US just right of centre · Brazil, France, Italy · Russia, India, China · Nigeria at the top-down end.
   - The Country Mapping 2022 differs in detail (e.g. Brazil vs US).
   - Positions are **relative** generalizations (predominant tendencies, tentative hypotheses), not stereotypes.
5. **Recommendations.**
   - *Consensual:* expect it to take longer; show patience and commitment; check in; cultivate informal contacts; don't push, focus on quality.
   - *Top-down:* expect the boss to decide; follow even if overruled; when in charge, listen but decide quickly; if divided with no leader, vote; stay flexible.
   - *Mixed teams:* agree the decision method up front (vote or boss? 100%? deadline? flexibility?), revisit it before big decisions, and ask **"small d or big D?"**
   - Ready-made exam examples: the US–German merger, Per's inbox, Sheldon in Tokyo.
